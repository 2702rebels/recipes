---
title: Five Mechanisms to Tune
description: Why almost every FRC mechanism tunes like a flywheel, turret, arm, elevator, or linear deployment, how to tell which one you have, and the cases that need extra care.
---

Robots look different every year, but their motors see only a few kinds of load. From the controller's point of view, almost every mechanism is one of five types: a **flywheel**, a **turret**, an **arm**, an **elevator**, or a **linear deployment**. Once you know which type a mechanism is, you know which gains it needs and which [tuning procedure](/docs/guides/ctre/procedures) to follow. In short:

1. **Ask three questions.** Does the controller hold a speed or a position? How does gravity load the motor: not at all, by the same amount everywhere, or by an amount that changes with angle? Is the travel long, or short with hard stops?
2. **The answers pick the type.** Speed control is a flywheel. Position with no gravity is a turret, with a constant gravity load an elevator, and with a load that changes with angle an arm. Short travel between hard stops is a linear deployment.
3. **The type picks the gains.** Arms and elevators need kG for gravity: a cosine for an arm, a constant for an elevator. Turrets, arms, and elevators need kD and usually a motion profile. Flywheels need neither.
4. **Some mechanisms need extra care.** A wrist on an arm, a telescoping arm, or a spring-balanced mechanism doesn't fit one type exactly. Start from the closest type and add what's missing.

## 6.1 The five types

<MechanismTypes />

| Type | Controller holds | Gravity load on the motor | Gains that matter | Examples |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Flywheel** | Speed | None | kS, kV, kP (and kA for spin-up) | Shooter wheels, intake and indexer rollers, swerve drive wheels |
| **Turret** | Angle | None | kS, kV, kA, kP, kD | Turrets, swerve steering |
| **Arm** | Angle | Changes with angle: largest when horizontal, zero when vertical | kG (cosine), kS, kV, kA, kP, kD | Arms, wrists, shooter pivots, intake pivots, hoods |
| **Elevator** | Height | The same at every height | kG (constant), kS, kV, kA, kP, kD | Elevators, linear climbers, telescoping stages |
| **Linear deployment** | Position | Any of the above | Like an arm or elevator, but often just kG, kS, and kP | Intake slides, short intake pivots, climber hooks, hopper extensions |

The gain names come from the [gain slot model](/docs/guides/ctre/foundation#12-the-gain-slot-model): kS for friction, kV for speed, kA for acceleration, kG for gravity, and kP and kD for feedback. The table assumes voltage mode. In torque-current mode, kV is close to zero ([Feedforward in Torque Mode](/docs/guides/ctre/torque-current#303-feedforward-in-torque-mode)).

## 6.2 Why five are enough

Every mechanism driven by a motor obeys the same balance. The motor's torque goes into speeding the mechanism up, and into overcoming friction and gravity:

$$
J\,\ddot{\theta} = \tau_{\text{motor}} - \tau_{\text{friction}} - \tau_{\text{gravity}}(\theta)
$$

Term by term:

- $J\,\ddot{\theta}$ is **inertia** times **acceleration**. $J$ is how hard the mechanism is to speed up: its mass and how far from the axle that mass sits. For an elevator, use mass and linear acceleration instead. The feedforward gain kA covers this term.
- $\tau_{\text{motor}}$ is the motor's torque, after the gearbox. In voltage mode, part of the voltage goes to cancel the motor's back-EMF, which grows with speed. That is what kV covers ([Voltage Control](/docs/guides/ctre/voltage)).
- $\tau_{\text{friction}}$ is friction in bearings, gears, and belts. It is roughly constant and always opposes motion. kS covers it.
- $\tau_{\text{gravity}}(\theta)$ is the torque gravity puts on the motor. kG covers it.

The first three terms look the same on every mechanism. Only the gravity term changes, and it comes in three shapes:

| Gravity term | When | Type |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Zero** | The mechanism turns about a vertical axis (a turret), or it is balanced about its axle, so gravity pulls equally on both sides (a flywheel) | Flywheel or turret |
| **Constant** | The mechanism moves in a straight line, up or at a fixed tilt | Elevator |
| **Cosine of the angle** | The mechanism rotates in a vertical plane | Arm |

The rest of the difference is **what the controller holds**. A flywheel holds a speed, so it needs kV and doesn't care where it is. A turret, arm, or elevator holds a position, so it needs kP to pull back to that position and usually kD to settle there.

The fifth type, the **linear deployment**, is not new physics. It is an arm or an elevator with short travel between hard stops. Short moves are almost all speeding up and slowing down, so a simpler tune is often enough ([Linear Deployment](/docs/guides/ctre/voltage#25-linear-deployment-position)).

## 6.3 Which type is it?

Go down the table. The first row that fits the mechanism gives its type:

| If the mechanism... | Type |
|:---|:---|
| Holds a speed | **Flywheel** |
| Moves a short way between hard stops, to only a few positions | **Linear deployment** |
| Holds a position, and gravity doesn't load the motor | **Turret** |
| Holds a position, and gravity loads the motor the same everywhere | **Elevator** |
| Holds a position, and gravity's load changes with angle | **Arm** |

Mechanisms from recent games, sorted by type:

| Type | Mechanism | Game | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Flywheel** | Shooter flywheels | 2022, 2024, 2026 | Recovery after each shot matters most |
| **Flywheel** | Intake, indexer, and end effector rollers | Every year | Usually run open loop at a set voltage |
| **Flywheel** | Spindexer, rolling floor, or dye rotor | 2026 | Spins at a set speed. Often run open loop |
| **Flywheel** | Swerve drive wheels | Every year | Speed control. The traction limit caps the current ([Swerve Tuning](/docs/guides/swerve)) |
| **Turret** | Turret | 2022, 2026 | Watch for cable limits. Most turrets can't spin forever |
| **Turret** | Swerve steering | Every year | Wraps around continuously ([Swerve Tuning](/docs/guides/swerve)) |
| **Arm** | Arm | 2023 | Its kG grows with what it carries |
| **Arm** | Pivot on an elevator carriage | 2025 | Gravity acts the same whatever the elevator's height |
| **Arm** | Pivoting shooter | 2024 | Heavy, with the flywheels far from the axle |
| **Arm** | Climber on a pivot | 2025 | Pulls the whole robot. Pull with a set current |
| **Arm**, or linear deployment | Hood | 2022, 2026 | A light hood with short travel tunes like a deployment |
| **Arm**, or linear deployment | Intake pivot | Every year | Often only two positions: in and out |
| **Elevator** | Elevator | 2025 | Check the rigging. Some elevators need a different kG for each stage |
| **Elevator** | Climber on a lift | 2024, 2026 | Pulls the whole robot. Pull with a set current |
| **Linear deployment** | Intake slide | 2026 | Drives out to a hard stop, and back in |
| **Linear deployment** | Expanding hopper | 2026 | Often only needs to reach a hard stop |

## 6.4 Mechanisms that need extra care

Some mechanisms don't fit one type exactly. Start from the closest type, then handle what's different:

| Mechanism | What's different | What to do |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Wrist on an arm** | Gravity acts on the wrist's angle from horizontal, which is the arm's angle plus the wrist's angle. The wrist's own encoder only knows its angle relative to the arm | Compute the gravity feedforward in code from both angles and pass it with the request ([Compound Arms](/docs/guides/ctre/torque-current#334-compound-arms-multi-joint)) |
| **Two-joint arm** | Each joint's gravity load depends on the other joint's angle | Same as a wrist on an arm |
| **Telescoping arm** | The pivot's gravity load grows as the arm extends. The extension's gravity load changes with the pivot's angle | Schedule kG by extension (switch gain slots), or compute the feedforward in code |
| **Tilted elevator** | Gravity pulls along the rails with only part of its strength | Still an elevator. kG is just smaller |
| **Spring- or gas-spring-balanced** | The spring force changes with position, so the leftover load is neither constant nor a cosine | Measure the hold at several positions with the bracketing test. If it changes a lot, schedule kG or compute it in code |
| **Holding a game piece** | A heavy piece raises kG and kA | Measure kG with and without the piece. Switch gain slots when the robot picks one up |
| **Differential mechanism** | Two motors work together to drive two joints, as in some wrists | Convert motor motion to joint motion first. Each joint is then one of the five types |
